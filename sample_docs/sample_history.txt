The Second World War: Origins, Turning Points, and Lasting Legacy

The Second World War, the deadliest conflict in human history, resulted in an estimated 70 to 85 million casualties and fundamentally reshaped the political, economic, and social order of the world. The war, which lasted from September 1, 1939, to September 2, 1945, was fought across Europe, Asia, Africa, and the Pacific, drawing in virtually every major nation on earth.

The roots of the conflict lay in the aftermath of the First World War and the deeply punitive Treaty of Versailles, signed at the Palace of Versailles near Paris, France on June 28, 1919. Germany was stripped of territory, disarmed, and forced to pay enormous reparations. The resulting economic hardship and national humiliation created fertile ground for the rise of Adolf Hitler and the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP), which came to power in January 1933. Hitler was appointed Chancellor of Germany by President Paul von Hindenburg on January 30, 1933, and consolidated total dictatorial power by August 1934.

Germany's annexation of Austria — known as the Anschluss — took place in March 1938, followed by the occupation of the Sudetenland region of Czechoslovakia, permitted by the Munich Agreement signed by British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain, French Premier Édouard Daladier, Italian dictator Benito Mussolini, and Hitler in Munich, Germany on September 29, 1938. The agreement became synonymous with the failed policy of appeasement.

World War II officially began when Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939. Britain and France declared war on Germany two days later. Soviet forces invaded Poland from the east on September 17, 1939, following the secret Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact signed between German Foreign Minister Joachim von Ribbentrop and Soviet Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov in Moscow on August 23, 1939.

The fall of France in June 1940 was a stunning German military achievement. Paris was occupied on June 14, 1940, and an armistice was signed at Compiègne on June 22. General Charles de Gaulle fled to London and broadcast his famous appeal to French resistance on BBC Radio on June 18, 1940. Winston Churchill, who had replaced Chamberlain as British Prime Minister in May 1940, rallied the nation with his famous speeches: "We shall fight on the beaches" and "Their finest hour."

Operation Barbarossa — Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union — began on June 22, 1941, the largest military operation in history with over 3.8 million Axis troops advancing on a 2,900-kilometre front. The city of Leningrad (now Saint Petersburg) endured a siege lasting 872 days from September 1941 to January 1944. The Battle of Stalingrad, fought between August 1942 and February 1943, marked a critical turning point: German Field Marshal Friedrich Paulus surrendered his 6th Army of 300,000 men to Soviet Marshal Georgy Zhukov, marking the first major German defeat.

The United States entered the war following the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor, Hawaii on December 7, 1941 — described by President Franklin D. Roosevelt as "a date which will live in infamy." Roosevelt, Winston Churchill, and Soviet Premier Joseph Stalin formed the core of the Allied leadership. The three leaders met at the Tehran Conference in Tehran, Iran in November 1943, and again at the Yalta Conference in Yalta, Ukraine (then Soviet Union) in February 1945.

The D-Day landings on June 6, 1944 — codenamed Operation Overlord — saw Allied forces under Supreme Commander General Dwight D. Eisenhower land on five beaches in Normandy, France. Over 156,000 American, British, and Canadian troops participated in the largest seaborne invasion in history. General Omar Bradley commanded US ground forces, while Field Marshal Bernard Montgomery commanded overall Allied ground forces.

The Holocaust, the systematic genocide of six million Jewish people orchestrated by Nazi Germany, took place primarily between 1941 and 1945. Major extermination camps including Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, and Sobibór were located in occupied Poland. The Nuremberg Trials, held in Nuremberg, Germany from November 1945 to October 1946, prosecuted senior Nazi officials. Chief US Prosecutor Robert H. Jackson presented evidence before the International Military Tribunal.

The Pacific War ended following the atomic bombings of Hiroshima on August 6, 1945, and Nagasaki on August 9, 1945, ordered by US President Harry S. Truman. Japan's formal surrender was signed aboard USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay on September 2, 1945. The United Nations was established on October 24, 1945, in San Francisco, California, with the original 51 member nations committed to preventing future global conflicts. Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the UN Commission on Human Rights, which drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted in Paris on December 10, 1948.
